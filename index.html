English research brief

Hiragana and katakana: sounds, history, and the proposed additions

This tab combines everyday kana, historical forms, and examples from a modern encoding proposal. Read the chart as a comparison of forms: its filled positions do not mean that every sound was distinguished in every period.

What the sources say

Two scripts for Japanese sounds

Hiragana and katakana developed from Chinese characters. Hiragana commonly writes grammatical endings, particles, and Japanese words; katakana commonly writes borrowed words and provides emphasis. Their basic sound inventories overlap. Historical variants and changes in pronunciation explain why a modern chart does not tell the whole story. Unicode documents archaic hiragana YE separately from ordinary modern E. Source: Unicode, East Asia chapter.

Why propose “missing” kana?

Abraham Gross’s January 2020 proposal argues for encoding additional forms so historical and specialist material can be represented as text. It presents hiragana YI and WU, katakana YI, YE, WU, and small WU, with glyphs and supporting examples. This is an argument about representing written material; it should be read alongside the examples, dates, and proposed assignments in the document. The images displayed here preserve that proposal’s forms. Source: original proposal.

How to read the sound grid

A mora is a timing unit: kya counts as one, while a long vowel occupies two. The familiar “fifty sounds” arrangement is a five-vowel grid with historical gaps, rather than a count of fifty distinct sounds in contemporary Japanese. A small display form is also a different question from a separately encoded character. The small YI/YE images in this project are reduced display variants.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

The catalog retains the original proposal’s suggested code points for comparison. Later assignments differ: hiragana archaic WU is U+1B11F; katakana archaic YI, YE, and WU are U+1B120, U+1B121, and U+1B122. Hiragana archaic YE is U+1B001. Consult the current charts when entering actual Unicode text, rather than copying the proposal’s suggested numbers. Kana Extended-A chart · Kana Supplement chart.

Source register. The bundled proposal supplies the catalog artwork and its proposed assignments. Unicode’s specification and code charts, added here for context, document the script and standardized assignments. Encoding records answer which characters can be represented; the historical examples address how particular forms were used.

Questions for further research. For each unusual form, which dated publication uses it? Does that author describe a distinct pronunciation, a spelling convention, or a chart-completion device? Comparing those questions across periods is more informative than treating every filled cell as the same kind of evidence.

English research brief

Woshite: a sound system and a cosmological reading of writing

Woshite is also written Wosite. Its distinctive feature is the relationship its interpreters draw between the construction of a sign, its sound, and processes in nature. The table above lets you compare the forms before exploring that interpretation.

What the sources say

Shapes as processes

WoshiteWorld presents 48 signs arranged by five vowels and consonant modifiers. In its explanation, the vowel frames correspond to space, wind, fire, water, and soil energies. Consonant elements describe further changes of state; a dot marks an unmodified vowel. Writing therefore carries an account of creation as well as sound. The author describes the system as ancient and deliberately organized, and proposes that studying its signs reveals unseen creative energies behind matter and life. Source: Wosite Syllabary.

Readings and the people behind the interpretation

The same article identifies an exceptional RU form and calls the W/U-position sign wu, while noting its present reading as n. It credits Yoshinosuke Matsumoto with establishing the syllabary through study, and Mitsuru Ikeda and the Japan Woshite Institute with developing interpretations of the signs. Its illustrations are attributed to Aoki and Hiraoka’s 2009 Yomigaeru Nihongo. These details identify the interpretive lineage being summarized here. Source: article and illustration credits.

Reading the comparison table

The kana beneath each image is a reading label. The displayed chart places WI and WE in the Y-family positions and N in the W/U position. Comparing that arrangement with the practitioner’s WU explanation reveals a difference in labeling that readers should retain when comparing charts. A shared five-vowel layout alone does not identify two scripts as the same system.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Source register: the glyph chart. MOTOI Kenkichi’s Japanese Wosite Base 48 table is the source of this tab’s individual images. The Commons record identifies a modern drawing and gives its authorship and references. This website crops that chart into padded cells; the source image is a graphical reference rather than a photographed manuscript.

Source register: the interpretation. WoshiteWorld’s article provides the sound-and-energy explanation above. Its account of ancient design and cosmic meaning belongs in the claims section; its named researchers, publication credit, and reading conventions belong in the documentation record. These are different kinds of information even when found on one page.

Source register: reuse terms. The chart and these adapted glyph images use Creative Commons Attribution–ShareAlike 3.0. That link describes attribution and sharing conditions; it makes no claim about the script’s age or meaning.

Questions for further research. Which manuscript witness supports each form and reading? What are its date, custody history, and relationship to published reconstructions? How do different interpreters derive meanings from the same graphical elements? Keeping those records alongside the cosmological account allows readers to compare both.

English research brief

Katakamuna: 48 signs, 80 utahi, and several interpretive traditions

The circular compositions are utahi, numbered verses made with a 48-sign inventory. The claims below concern their origin and meaning. The separate documentation section records who publishes each explanation and what the linked material contains.

What the sources say

Yoshino Nobuko’s account

Yoshino describes the 80 verses as statements of universal laws. She reads kata as the visible, kamu as the invisible, and na as their unifying core. Inner thought is said to shape outer reality; her discussion connects the Japanese words for thought and heaviness with gravity. She reads verses 5–6 as a complete, unrepeated sequence of 48 sounds, associating five and six with i and ma: ima, “now.” Her historical narrative links Katakamuna to a remote civilization and interprets mythology, including Amaterasu and Susanoo, as clues to its fate. She also discusses Mu/Lemuria. These are parts of her explanation, rather than captions common to every diagram. Source: Yoshino’s introduction.

The preservation association’s account

The Katakamuna Preservation Association / Senshō Physics Society describes an approximately 20,000-year-old insight into the forces behind life and the cosmos, expressed through 48 sounds and 80 verses. It presents Narasaki Satsuki and Uno Tamie as the interpreters of this senshō, or latent, physics. Its discovery narrative places Narasaki near Mount Rokko in 1949, where Hira Tōji showed him a document. The association emphasizes Uno’s succession from Narasaki and learning through her publications and cultivated intuitive perception. Representative Egawa Kazuko describes assisting with Uno’s manuscripts and publishing inherited material. This lineage account has its own emphasis and should be read alongside Yoshino’s. Source: preservation association.

The 80-verse publisher’s interpretation

The linked verse index groups 1–4 as an introductory declaration; 5–6 as sound-concepts of life’s emergence; 7–17 as hitotsukata, shared patterns in living phenomena; 18–30 as ikatsu, glossed there as electron particles; 31–47 as emergence from latent to manifest; 48–63 as ikikokoro, an invisible whole of life; and 64–80 as fundamental principles of life. These are the editor’s thematic divisions. Source: complete verse index.

The individual verse pages also repeat a publisher description invoking Atlantis/Mu, a fractal manifest–latent pairing, and the generation of life through kamuna and amana. This recurring description frames the collection; it is not a separate translation of each verse. Pages 4–6 additionally connect their text with concepts such as projection, circulation, differentiation, and opposing processes. Individual pages are linked in the 80-source register below.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Source register: character artwork. Katakamuna / ILLUSTRATION＊STORE supplies the displayed 48 character images and additional named motifs. The creator describes producing the artwork with Affinity Designer 2 in 2023. These are modern reference graphics. A motif’s name, a sound sign, and a complete utahi are different items to track.

Source register: circular diagrams. Hajime’s collection provides the external 80-page visual document used by the reader. The numbered links connect a displayed page with a verse number. A diagram, its kana transcription, and an English interpretation should each retain their own source.

Source register: all 80 individual pages. Every page already linked in the table below was retrieved for this review, along with the collection index. They provide verse-specific transcriptions, sometimes alternate readings or commentary, and recurring publisher text. The register preserves all 80 direct references. The thematic overview above follows the index; it is not presented as eighty word-for-word English translations.

Source register: explanations and lineage. Yoshino’s introduction and the preservation association’s website are both sources for claims and for their authors’ accounts of transmission. Agreement between them, or their use of words such as energy and physics, is a subject to document separately from a dated manuscript or a measured result.

Questions for further research. Where can the earliest copies and transcriptions be examined? Which readings come from Narasaki, Uno, Yoshino, or later editors? For a proposed physical effect, what procedure, observations, and independent repetitions are supplied? For a historical date, what object or record is being dated? These questions leave each claim available for readers to investigate on its own terms.
