English research brief

Hiragana and katakana: sounds, history, and the proposed additions

This tab combines everyday kana, historical forms, and examples from a modern encoding proposal. Read the chart as a comparison of forms: its filled positions do not mean that every sound was distinguished in every period.

What the sources say

Two scripts for Japanese sounds

Hiragana and katakana developed from Chinese characters. Hiragana commonly writes grammatical endings, particles, and Japanese words; katakana commonly writes borrowed words and provides emphasis. Their basic sound inventories overlap. Historical variants and changes in pronunciation explain why a modern chart does not tell the whole story. Unicode documents archaic hiragana YE separately from ordinary modern E. Source: Unicode, East Asia chapter.

Why propose “missing” kana?

Abraham Gross’s January 2020 proposal argues for encoding additional forms so historical and specialist material can be represented as text. It presents hiragana YI and WU, katakana YI, YE, WU, and small WU, with glyphs and supporting examples. This is an argument about representing written material; it should be read alongside the examples, dates, and proposed assignments in the document. The images displayed here preserve that proposal’s forms. Source: original proposal.

How to read the sound grid

A mora is a timing unit: kya counts as one, while a long vowel occupies two. The familiar “fifty sounds” arrangement is a five-vowel grid with historical gaps, rather than a count of fifty distinct sounds in contemporary Japanese. A small display form is also a different question from a separately encoded character. The small YI/YE images in this project are reduced display variants.

Legends: Kūkai, the Iroha, and Kibi no Makibi

A widespread story credited Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi) with creating hiragana and the Iroha poem. NINJAL’s account explains how the poem’s Buddhist theme and its role as a writing model helped that attribution spread. Katakana was likewise attributed to the scholar Kibi no Makibi. These stories give the scripts celebrated cultural founders. Source: Yada Tsutomu, NINJAL language questions.

Dating record, separate from the story. The same account places the Iroha in the eleventh century, after Kūkai’s lifetime (774–835), and describes hiragana as a collective development rather than one person’s invention. The age of a script and the age of a story about its creator need their own records.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Later assignments differ: hiragana archaic WU is U+1B11F; katakana archaic YI, YE, and WU are U+1B120, U+1B121, and U+1B122. Hiragana archaic YE is U+1B001. Consult the current charts when entering actual Unicode text, rather than copying the proposal’s suggested numbers. Kana Extended-A chart · Kana Supplement chart.

Questions for further research. For each unusual form, which dated publication uses it? Does that author describe a distinct pronunciation, a spelling convention, or a chart-completion device? Comparing those questions across periods is more informative than treating every filled cell as the same kind of evidence.

English research brief

Woshite: a sound system and a cosmological reading of writing

Woshite is also written Wosite. Its distinctive feature is the relationship its interpreters draw between the construction of a sign, its sound, and processes in nature. The table above lets you compare the forms before exploring that interpretation.

What the sources say

Shapes as processes

WoshiteWorld presents 48 signs arranged by five vowels and consonant modifiers. In its explanation, the vowel frames correspond to space, wind, fire, water, and soil energies. Consonant elements describe further changes of state; a dot marks an unmodified vowel. Writing therefore carries an account of creation as well as sound. The author describes the system as ancient and deliberately organized, and proposes that studying its signs reveals unseen creative energies behind matter and life. Source: Wosite Syllabary.

Readings and the people behind the interpretation

The same article identifies an exceptional RU form and calls the W/U-position sign wu, while noting its present reading as n. It credits Yoshinosuke Matsumoto with establishing the syllabary through study, and Mitsuru Ikeda and the Japan Woshite Institute with developing interpretations of the signs. Its illustrations are attributed to Aoki and Hiraoka’s 2009 Yomigaeru Nihongo. These details identify the interpretive lineage being summarized here. Source: article and illustration credits.

Reading the comparison table

The kana beneath each image is a reading label. The displayed chart places WI and WE in the Y-family positions and N in the W/U position. Comparing that arrangement with the practitioner’s WU explanation reveals a difference in labeling that readers should retain when comparing charts. A shared five-vowel layout alone does not identify two scripts as the same system.

Legends: Izanagi, Izanami, and the Awauta

A practitioner account of the Awauta describes a song containing 48 sounds, beginning with A and ending with WA. In the story, Izanagi and Izanami travel and sing its complementary halves to restore clear speech and social order. Sound is consequently a means of bringing people and the world into harmony, not merely a label for an object. Source: Awauta no Shirabe.

The same account presents the circular Motoake / Futomani arrangement as divine seats and a pattern of creation and return. This helps explain why a sound inventory can also appear as a cosmological diagram. A Woshite Futomani arrangement and a similarly named diagram in another tradition should each retain their own reading key.

Documentation record. This source is a present-day practitioner’s explanation of the associated narratives. The legendary events, the transmitted texts, and the publication presenting them are separate stages to investigate.

This website crops that chart into padded cells; the source image is a graphical reference rather than a photographed manuscript.

Source register: the interpretation. WoshiteWorld’s article provides the sound-and-energy explanation above. Its account of ancient design and cosmic meaning belongs in the claims section; its named researchers, publication credit, and reading conventions belong in the documentation record. These are different kinds of information even when found on one page.

Source register: reuse terms. The chart and these adapted glyph images use Creative Commons Attribution–ShareAlike 3.0. That link describes attribution and sharing conditions; it makes no claim about the script’s age or meaning.

Questions for further research. Which manuscript witness supports each form and reading? What are its date, custody history, and relationship to published reconstructions? How do different interpreters derive meanings from the same graphical elements? Keeping those records alongside the cosmological account allows readers to compare both.

English research brief

Katakamuna: 48 signs, 80 utahi, and several interpretive traditions

The circular compositions are utahi, numbered verses made with a 48-sign inventory. The claims below concern their origin and meaning. The separate documentation section records who publishes each explanation and what the linked material contains.

What the sources say

Yoshino Nobuko’s account

Yoshino describes the 80 verses as statements of universal laws. She reads kata as the visible, kamu as the invisible, and na as their unifying core. Inner thought is said to shape outer reality; her discussion connects the Japanese words for thought and heaviness with gravity. She reads verses 5–6 as a complete, unrepeated sequence of 48 sounds, associating five and six with i and ma: ima, “now.” Her historical narrative links Katakamuna to a remote civilization and interprets mythology, including Amaterasu and Susanoo, as clues to its fate. She also discusses Mu/Lemuria. These are parts of her explanation, rather than captions common to every diagram. Source: Yoshino’s introduction.

The preservation association’s account

The Katakamuna Preservation Association / Senshō Physics Society describes an approximately 20,000-year-old insight into the forces behind life and the cosmos, expressed through 48 sounds and 80 verses. It presents Narasaki Satsuki and Uno Tamie as the interpreters of this senshō, or latent, physics. Its discovery narrative places Narasaki near Mount Rokko in 1949, where Hira Tōji showed him a document. The association emphasizes Uno’s succession from Narasaki and learning through her publications and cultivated intuitive perception. Representative Egawa Kazuko describes assisting with Uno’s manuscripts and publishing inherited material. This lineage account has its own emphasis and should be read alongside Yoshino’s. Source: preservation association.

The 80-verse publisher’s interpretation

The linked verse index groups 1–4 as an introductory declaration; 5–6 as sound-concepts of life’s emergence; 7–17 as hitotsukata, shared patterns in living phenomena; 18–30 as ikatsu, glossed there as electron particles; 31–47 as emergence from latent to manifest; 48–63 as ikikokoro, an invisible whole of life; and 64–80 as fundamental principles of life. These are the editor’s thematic divisions. Source: complete verse index.

The individual verse pages also repeat a publisher description invoking Atlantis/Mu, a fractal manifest–latent pairing, and the generation of life through kamuna and amana. This recurring description frames the collection; it is not a separate translation of each verse. Pages 4–6 additionally connect their text with concepts such as projection, circulation, differentiation, and opposing processes. Individual pages are linked in the 80-source register below.

Legends: the guarded scroll and a lost civilization

In the preservation association’s discovery account, Hira Tōji, described as connected with a Katakamuna shrine, shows Narasaki a scroll during his 1949 research near Mount Rokko. Narasaki copies its unfamiliar signs. The guardian and scroll form the bridge between a remote civilization and the modern transmission narrative. Source: preservation association.

Yoshino reads Amaterasu’s cave story and Susanoo through her explanation of Katakamuna, and discusses Mu/Lemuria as part of its remote background. This is her interpretation of myth, alongside the verse publisher’s Atlantis/Mu framing described above. Yoshino’s account.

Documentation record. A date given for the modern encounter, an attributed prehistoric date, and the publication date of a retelling answer different questions. The reader can trace these accounts without treating every retelling as an independently recovered manuscript.

Hentaigana

English research brief · 変体仮名

Many shapes for the same sound

What the sources describe

Hentaigana are historical alternatives within the hiragana tradition. A reading can have several forms because writers used different parent kanji, or developed different cursive shapes from the same kanji. They are useful for reading handwritten books, signs, names, and calligraphy. A catalog of forms therefore has more entries than a chart of sounds. Unicode’s account of historical kana.

The National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics (NINJAL) catalogs each form by a research identifier, reading, parent character, and, where available, Unicode and MJ identifiers. Its guide also identifies reference dictionaries and historical type specimens used for comparison. A research identifier describes a catalog entry; a Unicode number identifies an encoded character. They are related records, not interchangeable numbering systems. NINJAL catalog guide.

Legends and cultural associations

As part of the hiragana tradition, these forms share the story that Kūkai, or Kōbō Daishi, created kana and the Iroha poem. That is a story about the script’s origin and authority, rather than a separate creator legend for each variant. See the kana tab’s legend section for the account and its dating discussion. This catalog does not assign a deity or spiritual power to each hentaigana.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

The result contains 286 encoded forms, counting archaic YE as E-1, and 28 additional catalog forms with no code point listed there. Each entry links to its institute record or the relevant Unicode chart. “No assignment listed” reports this source’s field; it is not a claim that the form can never be encoded.

English research brief · 万葉仮名

Writing Japanese sounds with kanji

What the sources describe

Man’yōgana uses Chinese characters to represent Japanese sounds. The educational resource Tanoshii Man’yōshū distinguishes borrowing a character’s Sino-Japanese sound from borrowing a Japanese reading, and illustrates how more than one character can serve one sound. Its examples connect this practice with the later development of kana. Source: introduction and example list.

The table selects two examples for each of the source’s 45 non-N reading groups. It is an entry point, not a complete Man’yōgana dictionary. It omits voiced series and historical WI/WE examples, and groups examples under modern kana. Old Japanese spelling distinctions need a more detailed table and a dated textual witness.

A scholarly study of Man’yōshū spelling points out that an inventory can change depending on whether it counts readings found only in proper names or poetic formulas. That is a practical reason to record a cited occurrence alongside a reading, rather than treat every listed character as freely interchangeable. Research discussion: scope of phonetic readings.

Legends and the language being recorded

Man’yōgana can be used to record poetry, personal names, and mythological names. The content of such a passage and the origin of the writing method are separate subjects. The sources used for this table do not supply a single divine-inventor legend for Man’yōgana. We have therefore not assigned one by analogy with the jindai-moji traditions. Compare the Kūkai and Kibi no Makibi stories in the kana tab.

A many-to-one relationship between characters and readings is central to this material; compressing it to one “correct” symbol per modern mora would lose information.

English research brief · 阿比留文字

Ahiru / Abiru: signs attributed to a transmitted tradition

What the chart records

The linked Jindai Moji Collection labels these signs Ahiru and also uses the name Mononobe. It supplies two 47-sign chart variants, with historical WI and WE and gaps at YI, YE, and WU. The first is displayed below; the second can be opened for comparison. The Latin labels belong to the source chart. Source chart.

Both variants belong to this catalog’s Ahiru entry. They should not be silently equated with every other chart called Mononobe, nor with the separately listed Ahiru cursive set. Names can travel between collections while the drawn forms differ.

Legends and transmission stories

A transmission account associates Ahiru writing with the Urabe and Abiru families of Tsushima. The family’s documents are said to preserve it alongside other character traditions. Comparative survey, Ahiru section.

The broader divine-writing narrative describes an original Japanese writing preserved through sacred lineages after Chinese writing became dominant. Kokugakuin’s historical account records Hirata Atsutane’s 1819 collection and his acceptance of two Hifumi forms, true and cursive, as divine writing. That story of preservation explains the authority claimed for the signs; it should remain distinguishable from the date of a particular copy. Mori Mizue, Encyclopedia of Shinto.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Graphical witness. The images below are the online collection’s small reference graphics, loaded from its publisher. They are not photographs of an ancient object. Each table links back to the exact chart. Their 47-sign inventory is retained rather than completed with invented forms.

Historical discussion. Kokugakuin also records arguments against divine-writing theories, including the relationship discussed between Hifumi and Hangul. This is part of the history of interpretation and debate. It is separate from the legend above and from a direct examination of a specific family document. Historical overview.

Further research. Locate a dated witness for each chart variant; record its owner, title, copyist, and reading key. Compare signs and their organization individually. A resemblance, a family attribution, and an uninterrupted chain of custody are three different kinds of information.

Reviewed 18 October 2026. Ahiru and Abiru are alternate romanizations used for this name.

English research brief · 阿比留草文字

Ahiru cursive: a separately cataloged form

What the chart records

The online collection calls this set Ahiru-kusa, Ahiru cursive, and Mononobe cursive. Its chart contains 47 sound signs, with WI and WE but no separate YI, YE, WU, or N. “Cursive” is a source label here: the chart alone does not demonstrate a step-by-step evolution from the block-like Ahiru signs. Source chart and names.

Keeping this set in its own tab allows the graphical relationship to be examined instead of assumed. A practical comparison should ask which strokes recur, whether the reading keys agree, and whether a dated document actually identifies the forms as variants.

Legends and sacred use

The comparative survey associates these forms with shrine seals and protective talismans, and reports several transmitted styles. Such accounts connect writing with ritual preservation as well as ordinary sound notation. Survey, Ahiru cursive section.

A public-domain chart reproduction identifies a Satsuma-associated tradition and cites Ochiai Naosumi’s Nihon kodai moji kō. This regional attribution should be retained alongside the Ahiru name, rather than replaced with a single universal origin story. Chart reproduction and source credit.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Reference used here. The table uses the online collection’s labeled glyph graphics. The Commons reproduction is an additional visual witness with its own source credit; it is not the artwork used in the cells below. This distinction lets readers check whether two publications really show the same form.

Tradition and chronology. A protective object documents a use of writing at the time the object was made. A narrative attributing that writing to the age of the gods makes a further claim about origin. Record both statements with their respective sources and dates. The linked survey describes a family of styles, so a single modern chart should not stand for every regional example.

Further research. Compare the online forms against a particular edition and page of Ochiai’s work, then seek the earlier object or manuscript that edition cites. Record whether WI/WE, voiced sounds, and small forms are explicitly supplied or inferred by a later editor.

English research brief · 豊国文字

Toyokuni: old-style and new-style inventories

What the charts record

The online collection publishes separate old-style and new-style Toyokuni charts. Each fills 50 positions, explicitly labeling YI, YE, and WU in addition to the familiar kana readings. Those labels are retained here as the chart’s assignments. They do not independently date the forms or establish fifty distinct sounds in every stage of Japanese. Old-style chart · New-style chart.

“Old” and “new” are the names of two source categories. Both are displayed, so readers can compare their construction without having to infer one from the other. This page does not merge them into a single glyph per sound.

Legends and transmission stories

Toyokuni writing is associated with Uetsufumi, said to have been transmitted through families in the Bungo region. The comparative survey also reports Tokumasa Kingo’s 1933 theory linking Toyokuni to Egypt and hieroglyphs. These are distinct transmission and origin narratives. Survey, Toyokuni section.

A diagrammatic resemblance to another writing system can be part of an author’s proposed explanation. To follow that explanation, keep its author, publication, comparison method, and proposed route of transmission attached to it. An Egyptian-origin theory should not become an anonymous caption for every Toyokuni chart.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Graphical sources. Each chart below loads the images from the corresponding page of the online collection. The source distinguishes kotai shōji and shintai shōji, old-style and new-style graphic signs. These pages provide a reading key but do not supply a manuscript photograph for each individual cell.

Wider historical context. Kokugakuin’s account places the appearance of Uetsufumi literature in the late Edo and Restoration periods. That statement concerns the appearance of the literature in the historical record; it is separate from the much earlier origins proposed within associated traditions. Encyclopedia of Shinto.

Further research. Identify which Uetsufumi manuscript or edition underlies each drawn chart. Compare missing or additional signs, reading keys, and voiced forms at the witness level. “Toyokuni” can identify a tradition without guaranteeing that every chart under that name has an identical inventory.

Izumo

Read the English brief, then explore the source-specific character reference below.

English research brief · 出雲文字

Izumo: a cave-inscription tradition and a 50-position chart

What the chart records

The reference collection titles its chart Izumo, Toyono, and Fumishima characters. It provides 50 positions, including signs labeled YI, YE, and WU. Those assignments are transcribed as labels beneath the glyphs here; we have not substituted modern kana into the three otherwise unfamiliar positions. Source chart.

The related names are useful search terms, but not a reason to merge this chart with Toyokuni. “Toyono” and “Toyokuni” need to be tracked as written in each source. The sound key and actual graphical forms are better identifiers than a similar romanized name.

The cave and its divine author

A transmitted account credits Ōnamuchi with creating the signs and describes inscriptions on a cave wall on Fumishima near Izumo Taisha. Tachibana Mitsuyoshi is said to have copied and transmitted them. Survey, Izumo section.

This story has several separately researchable parts: divine authorship, a physical location, a copying event, and subsequent transmission. A modern drawing can preserve a version of that tradition without being a direct image of the cave wall. Retaining those distinctions helps the legend remain legible without collapsing its stages into one claim.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Graphical witness. The cells below use the reference collection’s glyph graphics. Its chart gives labels and alternate names. This website is documenting that published chart, not presenting a new reading of an inscription.

Historical discussion. The comparative survey identifies a chart in Hirata’s supplementary discussion of divine characters. Kokugakuin’s broader history also records reports of transmitted Izumo material and disagreement among Edo-period scholars. Those records give routes into the documentary history; a region’s sacred reputation alone does not date a particular sign. Encyclopedia of Shinto.

Further research. Seek the earliest surviving copy attributed to Tachibana, the exact publication reproducing it, and any independent description of the site. Compare the order and labels of the copied forms with the convenient five-vowel grid shown here. A later rearrangement into a sound table is another editorial step to record.

Ryūtai

Read the English brief, then explore the source-specific character reference below.

English research brief · 龍体文字

Ryūtai: dragon writing and present-day interpretive practice

What contemporary sources claim

Tokuma Shoten’s description of Mori Michiyo’s 2024 book presents a 48-character system with spiritual powers and a Futomani arrangement. It assigns particular purposes to signs and pairs: for example, tsuru for gathering people, muku for spreading something, ku for financial fortune, and kini for health. These are the publication’s attributed interpretations, not meanings supplied by the chart labels below. Publisher-supplied book description.

A 2022 announcement from the Dragon Letter promotion organization describes workshops developed under Mori’s guidance, using 48 sounds and sixteen paired combinations. It presents automatic writing as part of her involvement. This identifies a contemporary teaching and dissemination network. Organization’s announcement.

A divine creator and a remote origin

The book description attributes Ryūtai to the deity Umashiashikabihikoji approximately 5,500 years ago and portrays it as a secret of the imperial household and a gift from a higher-dimensional world. Its Futomani discussion associates the arrangement with divine beings. These elements explain how the writing is situated within a sacred narrative. Source: book description and contents.

The promotion organization likewise identifies Umashiashikabihikoji as the creator. The presence of that deity’s name in a modern announcement documents the organization’s account; a separate question is where an early written witness for the attributed script can be examined. Source: organization’s statement.

Documentation and provenance — a separate record

Two inventories. The reference collection’s Tatsu / Ryūtai chart supplies the images below. It has 51 labeled positions: a full five-by-ten grid plus N. The contemporary book describes 48 characters. This website preserves the 51-position reference as a named chart variant; it does not claim it is Mori’s exact 48-sign set.

What has been consulted. The publisher’s public description and contents establish the book’s advertised claims and publication details. The full book has not been transcribed here. The organization’s public announcement establishes its own teaching claims. The image collection supplies a reading key, not a test of claimed effects or a dated ancient manuscript.

Further research. Compare the 48-sign teaching chart directly against this 51-position variant. Record exactly which forms differ or are omitted before transferring any sign-specific interpretation. For historical origin, locate the earliest manuscript and custody record; for claimed effects, distinguish a reported experience from a documented procedure and independent observations.
