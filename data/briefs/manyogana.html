Writing Japanese sounds with kanji

What the sources describe

Man’yōgana uses Chinese characters to represent Japanese sounds. The educational resource Tanoshii Man’yōshū distinguishes borrowing a character’s Sino-Japanese sound from borrowing a Japanese reading, and illustrates how more than one character can serve one sound. Its examples connect this practice with the later development of kana. Source: introduction and example list.

The table selects two examples for each of the source’s 45 non-N reading groups. It is an entry point, not a complete Man’yōgana dictionary. It omits voiced series and historical WI/WE examples, and groups examples under modern kana. Old Japanese spelling distinctions need a more detailed table and a dated textual witness.

A scholarly study of Man’yōshū spelling points out that an inventory can change depending on whether it counts readings found only in proper names or poetic formulas. That is a practical reason to record a cited occurrence alongside a reading, rather than treat every listed character as freely interchangeable. Research discussion: scope of phonetic readings.

Legends and the language being recorded

Man’yōgana can be used to record poetry, personal names, and mythological names. The content of such a passage and the origin of the writing method are separate subjects. The sources used for this table do not supply a single divine-inventor legend for Man’yōgana. We have therefore not assigned one by analogy with the jindai-moji traditions.
